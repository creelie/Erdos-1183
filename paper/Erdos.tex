\begin{abstract}
We prove the two Erd\H{o}s--Ulam conjectures on monochromatic
union-closed families. For every fixed exponent, each two-colouring of
the subsets of a large finite set contains a monochromatic union-closed
family larger than that power of the size of the set, while suitable
colourings keep every such family subexponential. The lower bound
combines a supersaturated Hales--Jewett theorem with chains of disjoint
blocks; for colourings by cardinality it becomes a statement about
monochromatic Hilbert cubes. Both proofs are checked in Lean.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Write $[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}$ and $2^{[n]}$ for the family of all subsets
of $[n]$. A \emph{$2$-colouring} is a map $\chi:2^{[n]}\to\{0,1\}$, and a
family $\cF\subseteq2^{[n]}$ is \emph{monochromatic} if $\chi$ is
constant on it. We call $\cF$ \emph{union-closed} if $A\cup B\in\cF$
whenever $A,B\in\cF$, and a \emph{sublattice} if it is closed under both
union and intersection. Erd\H{o}s and Ulam asked how large a
monochromatic family of either kind every colouring is forced to contain.
Accordingly let
\begin{align*}
f(n)&=\min_{\chi}\,\max\bigl\{|\cL|:\ \cL\subseteq2^{[n]}
  \text{ is a monochromatic sublattice}\bigr\},\\
F(n)&=\min_{\chi}\,\max\bigl\{|\cF|:\ \cF\subseteq2^{[n]}
  \text{ is a monochromatic union-closed family}\bigr\},
\end{align*}
the minima being taken over all $2$-colourings of $2^{[n]}$. A single set
is a sublattice, and every sublattice is union-closed, so
$1\le f(n)\le F(n)$. A maximal chain
$\emptyset\subset\{1\}\subset\{1,2\}\subset\dots\subset[n]$ has $n+1$
members, at least $\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil$ of them receive the same colour,
and every subchain is a sublattice. Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trivial}
F(n)\ \ge\ f(n)\ \ge\ \Bigl\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\Bigr\rceil .
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:lattice} shows the smallest interesting case.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-lattice.tex}
\caption{A $2$-colouring of the subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$, drawn on the
cube $2^{[3]}$. The five red sets (discs) form a union-closed family which
is not a sublattice, since $\{1\}\cap\{2\}=\emptyset$ is blue. The three
blue sets (squares) form a chain.}
\label{fig:lattice}
\end{figure}

The question is Problem~1183 in Bloom's collection of Erd\H{o}s problems
\cite{Bl26}, where it is listed as open. As recorded there, Erd\H{o}s
wrote that they had no plausible conjecture for the true order of
magnitude, but asked whether
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conj}
F(n)\ \ge\ n^{\omega(n)}\ \text{ for some }\omega(n)\to\infty,
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
F(n)\ <\ (1+o(1))^{n}.
\end{equation}
He also noted that Howorka had proved the first statement for colourings
in which the colour of a set depends only on its size. We prove both
statements in \eqref{eq:conj} for arbitrary colourings.

\subsection{Results}

The main result is a polynomial lower bound of every degree.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:lower}
For every integer $t\ge1$ there is a constant $c_t>0$ such that every
$2$-colouring of $2^{[n]}$ contains a monochromatic union-closed family
with at least $c_t\,n^{t+1}$ members; that is,
\[
F(n)\ \ge\ c_t\,n^{t+1}\qquad(n\ge1).
\]
One may take $c_t=\bigl(2\,t^{t}(t+2)^{M}(4M^{2})^{t+1}\bigr)^{-1}$,
where $M=M(t)$ is the number defined in \eqref{eq:Mt}.
\end{theorem}

The upper bound comes from a random colouring and the Sauer--Shelah
lemma.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:upper}
Let $n\ge1$, and let $d\ge1$ be an integer with $2^{d}>nd+2$. Then
\[
F(n)\ \le\ \sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{j}.
\]
In particular, for $n\ge32$,
\[
F(n)\ \le\ \bigl(\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n+2\bigr)\,
n^{\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n+1}\ =\ n^{(1+o(1))\log_2n}.
\]
\end{theorem}

Together they settle both parts of \eqref{eq:conj}, and they show that
the exponent $\omega(n)$ can never exceed $(1+o(1))\log_2n$.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:conj}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},leftmargin=2em]
\item There is a function $\omega:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$ with
$\omega(n)\to\infty$ such that $F(n)\ge n^{\omega(n)}$ for every
$n\ge1$.
\item There is a function $\eps:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$ with
$\eps(n)\to0$ such that $F(n)<(1+\eps(n))^{n}$ for every $n\ge1$.
\item Every function $\omega$ as in \textup{(i)} satisfies
$\omega(n)\le(1+o(1))\log_2n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

The two functions of Erd\H{o}s and Ulam turn out to be of quite
different sizes. For sublattices the trivial bound \eqref{eq:trivial} is
correct up to a factor of order $(\log n)^2$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:f}
For every $n\ge1$,
\[
\Bigl\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\Bigr\rceil\ \le\ f(n)\ \le\ n^{2}+n+1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
f(n)\ <\ 12\,n\,\bigl(\log_2(n+2)\bigr)^{2}.
\]
Thus $f(n)=n^{1+o(1)}$, whereas $F(n)/n^{C}\to\infty$ for every
constant $C$.
\end{theorem}

For small $n$ we have computed both functions exactly; $f(n)=\lceil
(n+1)/2\rceil$ for $n\le10$, and the values $F(1),\dots,F(7)$ are
$1,2,2,3,4,5,7$ (Section~\ref{sec:small}).

\subsection{Colourings by cardinality and Hilbert cubes}

Howorka's case of the problem is arithmetic in nature. Call $\chi$ a
\emph{cardinality colouring} if $\chi(S)=g(|S|)$ for some
$g:\{0,1,\dots,n\}\to\{0,1\}$. The building blocks of our families are
the \emph{$t$-cubes} $\{U\cup X_I:\ I\subseteq[t]\}$, where
$U,X_1,\dots,X_t$ are pairwise disjoint, the $X_i$ are nonempty and
$X_I=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_i$. The sizes of the members of such a cube form
the \emph{Hilbert cube}
\[
H(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)=\Bigl\{u+\sum_{i\in I}a_i:\ I\subseteq[t]\Bigr\},
\qquad u=|U|,\ a_i=|X_i|,
\]
so a cube is monochromatic under a cardinality colouring exactly when $g$
is constant on the corresponding Hilbert cube. Hilbert's lemma
\cite{Hi1892}, usually regarded as the first result of Ramsey theory,
says that every finite colouring of the positive integers has
monochromatic Hilbert cubes of every dimension. Read through this dictionary, the proof
of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} produces many monochromatic Hilbert cubes
spread along a single sequence of bounded steps.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:hilbert}
Let $t\ge1$ and $M=M(t)$, let $n\ge4M(t+1)$, and put
$m=\lfloor(n-1)/M\rfloor$ and $s=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$. For every
$g:\{0,1,\dots,n\}\to\{0,1\}$ there are integers
$\beta_1,\dots,\beta_m\in\{1,\dots,M\}$ and a colour $c$ with the
following property. For at least
\[
\frac{n^{t+1}}{2\,t^{t}\,(4M^{2})^{t+1}}
\]
of the pairs $(j,A)$ with $s<j\le m$, $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,s\}$ and
$|A|=t$, the function $g$ takes the value $c$ at every point of the
Hilbert cube
\[
H_\beta(j,A)=\Bigl\{\beta_1+\beta_2+\dots+\beta_j-\sum_{b\in B}\beta_b:\
B\subseteq A\Bigr\}.
\]
\end{theorem}

Different pairs $(j,A)$ may give the same set of integers; there are at
most $(n+1)M^{t}$ Hilbert cubes $H(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)$ in
$\{0,\dots,n\}$ with all $a_i\le M$. What
Theorem~\ref{thm:hilbert} provides is one sequence $\beta$ for which a
fixed proportion of the cubes indexed by the pairs $(j,A)$ have a common
colour, all of them hanging from the partial sums
$\beta_1+\dots+\beta_j$. This is the structure that lets the
corresponding families of sets be glued into one union-closed family. Section~\ref{sec:card} also gives a short
proof of Howorka's theorem from van der Waerden's theorem.

\subsection{The idea of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}}

Chains and cubes are monochromatic union-closed families that are easy to
find, but neither is large. A chain has at most $n+1$ members, and a
count of cubes shows that a random colouring has no monochromatic cube
with more than $O(n\log\log n)$ members. The families we build are unions of many cubes that
fit together. Take pairwise disjoint nonempty blocks $Y_1,\dots,Y_m$, put
$C_j=Y_1\cup\dots\cup Y_j$, and for $j>s$ and $A\subseteq[s]$ consider the
cube $Q(j,A)=\{C_j\setminus Y_B:\ B\subseteq A\}$. The union of a member
of $Q(j,A)$ and a member of $Q(j',A')$ with $j\le j'$ is again a member of
$Q(j',A')$, so the union of any collection of cubes $Q(j,A)$ that are
monochromatic in a common colour is union-closed. There are about
$m^{t+1}$ pairs $(j,A)$ with $|A|=t$, and it is therefore enough to make
a fixed proportion of these cubes monochromatic.

For a fixed chain of blocks this cannot be arranged, since the colouring
may have been chosen against it. We therefore randomise twice: the ground
set is permuted by a uniformly random $\sigma$, and each block size is
chosen at random from $\{1,\dots,M\}$. After a random permutation, the
probability that a cube is monochromatic depends only on the sizes of its
parts (Lemma~\ref{lem:shape}). A pigeonhole argument over permutations,
combined with the two-letter case of the multidimensional Hales--Jewett
theorem, shows that for every $r$ some shape whose base size lies between
$r$ and $r+M$ has probability at least $(t+2)^{-M}$ of being
monochromatic (Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation}). The shape of
$Q(j,A)$ is governed by $t+1$ block sizes, those of $Y_j$ and of $Y_a$ for
$a\in A$, and these can be tuned independently of all the others
(Lemma~\ref{lem:slices}). Averaging over $\sigma$ and the block sizes
then produces one chain of blocks in which a proportion
$(t+2)^{-M}M^{-t-1}$ of all the cubes is monochromatic
(Lemma~\ref{lem:average}). Figure~\ref{fig:proofmap} shows how the
pieces are assembled.

\subsection{Related work}

The union-closed families in this paper should not be confused with
those of Frankl's union-closed sets conjecture, which asks whether every
union-closed family other than $\{\emptyset\}$ has an element lying in at
least half of its members. That problem concerns the internal structure
of one family and involves no colouring. Gilmer \cite{Gi22} proved that
some element lies in a constant proportion of the members, and Alweiss,
Huang and Sellke \cite{AHS24} improved the constant. The entropy method
behind these results compares the union of two independent random
members of a family with a single random member, and we see no analogue
of it here, where the family is not given in advance.

Families of $2^{d}$ sets of the form $\{U\cup X_I\}$ are called
$d$-dimensional Boolean algebras by Gunderson, R\"odl and Sidorenko
\cite{GRS99}, who studied their extremal theory alongside that of
$d$-dimensional affine cubes of integers and complete $d$-partite
$d$-uniform hypergraphs. The passage between cubes of sets and Hilbert
cubes of integers used in Section~\ref{sec:card} is of the same kind. The
cube lemma of Section~\ref{sec:cube} is the two-letter case of the
multidimensional form of the Hales--Jewett theorem \cite{HJ63}; see
\cite{GRS90} for this form and for the background in Ramsey theory,
including van der Waerden's theorem \cite{vdW27}. The upper bound rests on the Sauer--Shelah lemma
\cite{Sa72,Sh72}, which is also at the root of the theory of
Vapnik--Chervonenkis dimension \cite{VC71}.

\subsection{Formal verification}

The definitions of $f$ and $F$, the lower bound \eqref{eq:trivial},
Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} in the form $F(n)\le\sum_{j<d}\binom nj$,

statements of \eqref{eq:conj} have been checked in the Lean proof

\section{Cubes and block chains}
\label{sec:cubes}

Throughout, $\chi$ is a $2$-colouring of $2^{[n]}$ and $S_n$ is the group
of permutations of $[n]$. A permutation acts on sets and on families of
sets in the obvious way: $\sigma(S)=\{\sigma(x):x\in S\}$ and
$\sigma(\cF)=\{\sigma(S):S\in\cF\}$. For sets $X_1,X_2,\dots$ and a finite
set $I$ of indices we write $X_I=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_i$, so that
$X_\emptyset=\emptyset$.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:cube}
Let $t\ge0$. A \emph{$t$-cube} in $2^{[n]}$ is a family
\[
Q(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)=\{U\cup X_I:\ I\subseteq[t]\},
\]
where $U,X_1,\dots,X_t\subseteq[n]$ are pairwise disjoint and
$X_1,\dots,X_t$ are nonempty. We call $U$ the \emph{base},
$T=U\cup X_{[t]}$ the \emph{top} and $X_1,\dots,X_t$ the
\emph{generators}. The \emph{shape} of the cube is the tuple
$(|U|;|X_1|,\dots,|X_t|)$.
\end{definition}

The $2^{t}$ sets $U\cup X_I$ are distinct, and
\[
(U\cup X_I)\cup(U\cup X_J)=U\cup X_{I\cup J},\qquad
(U\cup X_I)\cap(U\cup X_J)=U\cup X_{I\cap J},
\]
so a $t$-cube is a sublattice of $2^{[n]}$ isomorphic to $2^{[t]}$.
Writing $U\cup X_I=T\setminus X_{[t]\setminus I}$ describes the same
cube from the top downwards, as the family $\{T\setminus X_I:\
I\subseteq[t]\}$; this is the form in which cubes appear in block chains.
Figure~\ref{fig:holecube} shows a $3$-cube in this form.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-holecube.tex}
\caption{A $3$-cube with top $T$ and generators $X_1,X_2,X_3$, where
$X_{12}=X_1\cup X_2$ and so on. Moving along an edge in direction $i$
adds or removes $X_i$. The vertex $U=T\setminus X_{123}$ is the base.}
\label{fig:holecube}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:shape}
If $Q$ and $Q'$ are $t$-cubes in $2^{[n]}$ of the same shape, then
\[
\bigl|\{\sigma\in S_n:\ \sigma(Q)\text{ is monochromatic}\}\bigr|
=\bigl|\{\sigma\in S_n:\ \sigma(Q')\text{ is monochromatic}\}\bigr| .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $Q=Q(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)$ and $Q'=Q(U';X'_1,\dots,X'_t)$ with tops $T$
and $T'$. The sets $U,X_1,\dots,X_t,[n]\setminus T$ partition $[n]$, as do
$U',X'_1,\dots,X'_t,[n]\setminus T'$, and corresponding parts have the
same size. So there is $\tau\in S_n$ with $\tau(U)=U'$ and
$\tau(X_i)=X'_i$ for every $i$, and then $\tau(Q)=Q'$. Now
$\sigma(Q')=(\sigma\tau)(Q)$, and $\sigma\mapsto\sigma\tau$ is a bijection
of $S_n$.
\end{proof}

For integers $u\ge0$ and $a_1,\dots,a_t\ge1$ with
$u+a_1+\dots+a_t\le n$ we may therefore define
\[
\rho_\chi(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)=\frac{1}{n!}\,
\bigl|\{\sigma\in S_n:\ \sigma(Q)\text{ is monochromatic}\}\bigr|,
\]
where $Q$ is any $t$-cube of shape $(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)$. Since $\sigma(Q)$
is a uniformly random cube of that shape when $\sigma$ is uniform,
$\rho_\chi(u;a)$ is the probability that a random cube of shape $(u;a)$ is
monochromatic.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:chain}
Let $Y_1,\dots,Y_m\subseteq[n]$ be pairwise disjoint nonempty sets, the
\emph{blocks}, and put $C_j=Y_1\cup\dots\cup Y_j$ for $0\le j\le m$. For
$1\le j\le m$ and $A\subseteq[j-1]$ let
\[
Q(j,A)=\{C_j\setminus Y_B:\ B\subseteq A\}.
\]
This is the $|A|$-cube with top $C_j$, generators $Y_a$ $(a\in A)$ and base
$C_j\setminus Y_A$; its shape is
$\bigl(\sum_{k\le j,\,k\notin A}|Y_k|;\ (|Y_a|)_{a\in A}\bigr)$, the
generators being listed in increasing order of $a$.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:chain} shows a member of such a cube. The chain
structure enters through the following rule, illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:union}.

\centering
\input{figures/fig-chain.tex}

$\beta=(4,2,5,3,6,2,4,5,3,4)$. Here $s=5$, and the teal blocks form the

\begin{lemma}[Union rule]
\label{lem:union}
Let $1\le j\le j'\le m$ and $B,B'\subseteq[j]$. Then
\[
(C_j\setminus Y_B)\cup(C_{j'}\setminus Y_{B'})=C_{j'}\setminus
Y_{B\cap B'} .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both sides are contained in $C_{j'}$. Let $x\in C_{j'}$. If $x\notin
Y_{B'}$, then $x$ lies in $C_{j'}\setminus Y_{B'}$ and also outside
$Y_{B\cap B'}$. Otherwise $x\in Y_b$ for some $b\in B'$; then $b\le j$, so
$x\in C_j$, and $x$ lies in the left side if and only if $b\notin B$, that
is, if and only if $x\notin Y_{B\cap B'}$.
\end{proof}

\centering
\input{figures/fig-union.tex}
\caption{The union rule with $s=5$: $C_6\setminus Y_{\{2,4\}}$ and
$C_8\setminus Y_{\{3,4\}}$ have union $C_8\setminus Y_{\{4\}}$. Filled
squares are blocks present in the set.}
\label{fig:union}
\end{figure}

\begin{proposition}[Gluing]
\label{prop:glue}
Let $1\le s<m$, and let $\cS$ be a set of pairs $(j,A)$ with $s<j\le m$
and $A\subseteq[s]$ such that the cube $Q(j,A)$ is monochromatic for every
$(j,A)\in\cS$. Then there is a monochromatic union-closed family with at
least $|\cS|/2$ members.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Choose a colour $c$ such that the set $\cS_c$ of pairs in $\cS$ whose cube
has colour $c$ satisfies $|\cS_c|\ge|\cS|/2$, and let $\cF$ be the union
of the cubes $Q(j,A)$ over $(j,A)\in\cS_c$. Every member of $\cF$ has
colour $c$.

Let $C_j\setminus Y_B\in Q(j,A)$ and $C_{j'}\setminus Y_{B'}\in Q(j',A')$
be members of $\cF$, where $(j,A),(j',A')\in\cS_c$ and, say, $j\le j'$.
As $B,B'\subseteq[s]\subseteq[j]$, Lemma~\ref{lem:union} shows that
their union is $C_{j'}\setminus Y_{B\cap B'}$, and since $B\cap
B'\subseteq A'$ this set belongs to $Q(j',A')\subseteq\cF$. So $\cF$ is
union-closed.

Finally, the map $(j,A)\mapsto C_j\setminus Y_A$ from $\cS_c$ to $\cF$ is
injective. Indeed, $j>s$ means $j\notin A$, so $C_j\setminus Y_A$ meets
$Y_j$ and no block of larger index, which determines $j$; and then $A$ is
the set of indices $k\le s$ for which $Y_k$ is disjoint from
$C_j\setminus Y_A$. Hence $|\cF|\ge|\cS_c|\ge|\cS|/2$.
\end{proof}

The family $\cF$ is not closed under intersection in general: the
intersection of the two sets above is $C_j\setminus Y_{B\cup B'}$, and
$B\cup B'$ need not be contained in $A$. This is as it must be, in view of
Theorem~\ref{thm:f}.

\section{The cube lemma and supersaturation}
\label{sec:cube}

Define integers $M(t)$ by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Mt}
M(0)=0,\qquad M(t)=M(t-1)+2^{2^{M(t-1)}}\quad(t\ge1),
\end{equation}
so that $M(1)=2$, $M(2)=18$ and $M(3)=18+2^{2^{18}}$. Note that
$M(t)\ge t+1$ for $t\ge1$.

\begin{lemma}[Cube lemma]
\label{lem:hjcube}
Let $t\ge0$ and let $Z$ be a finite set with $|Z|\ge M(t)$. Every
$2$-colouring of $2^{Z}$ is constant on some $t$-cube $Q(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)$
with $U,X_1,\dots,X_t\subseteq Z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $t=0$ a single set, say $U=\emptyset$, will do. Let $t\ge1$, put
$q=M(t-1)$ and $N=2^{2^{q}}$, and choose disjoint sets $P,R\subseteq Z$ with
$|P|=N$ and $|R|=q$, which is possible as $|Z|\ge q+N$. List
$P=\{p_1,\dots,p_N\}$ and put $P_i=\{p_1,\dots,p_i\}$ for $0\le i\le N$.
Each $i$ gives a colouring $\varphi_i$ of $2^{R}$, namely
$\varphi_i(S)=\chi(P_i\cup S)$. There are only $N$ colourings of $2^{R}$
and $N+1$ indices, so $\varphi_a=\varphi_b$ for some $a<b$. By induction
$\varphi_a$ is constant, with value $c$ say, on a $(t-1)$-cube
$Q(U';X_1,\dots,X_{t-1})$ inside $R$. Put $U=P_a\cup U'$ and
$X_t=P_b\setminus P_a\neq\emptyset$. The sets $U,X_1,\dots,X_t$ are
pairwise disjoint, and for $I\subseteq[t-1]$
\begin{align*}
\chi(U\cup X_I)&=\varphi_a(U'\cup X_I)=c,\\
\chi(U\cup X_I\cup X_t)&=\varphi_b(U'\cup X_I)=\varphi_a(U'\cup X_I)=c .
\end{align*}
So $\chi$ is constant on $Q(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:hj}
Identify a set $S\subseteq Z$ with the word in $\{0,1\}^{Z}$ that has
the letter $1$ exactly at the positions in $S$. A $t$-cube then becomes a
$t$-dimensional combinatorial subspace: the positions in $U$ carry the
fixed letter $1$, those outside $T$ the fixed letter $0$, and those in
$X_i$ the $i$-th variable. Lemma~\ref{lem:hjcube} is therefore the
multidimensional form of the Hales--Jewett theorem \cite{HJ63} for the
alphabet $\{0,1\}$, with the bound \eqref{eq:Mt}. The formal proof described in
Section~\ref{sec:lean} uses the version of the Hales--Jewett theorem in
Mathlib, which covers every finite alphabet and gives some $M$ without an
explicit value; nothing else in the argument depends on the value of $M$.
\end{remark}

A single monochromatic cube is of little use by itself. What the proof
needs is a shape that is monochromatic with probability bounded below
independently of $n$, and this is obtained by applying
Lemma~\ref{lem:hjcube} once for every permutation.

\begin{proposition}[Supersaturation]
\label{prop:saturation}
Let $t\ge1$ and $M=M(t)$, and let $r\ge0$ be an integer with $r+M\le n$.
There are integers $u$ and $a_1,\dots,a_t\ge1$ with
\[
r\le u,\qquad (u-r)+a_1+\dots+a_t\le M,\qquad
\rho_\chi(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)\ \ge\ (t+2)^{-M}.
\]
In particular $u\le r+M-t$ and $a_i\le M$ for every $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $Z=[M]$ and $R=\{M+1,\dots,M+r\}$. For $\sigma\in S_n$ define a
colouring of $2^{Z}$ by $S\mapsto\chi(\sigma(S\cup R))$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:hjcube} gives disjoint $U,X_1,\dots,X_t\subseteq Z$ with
the $X_i$ nonempty such that this colouring is constant on
$Q(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)$; call $(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)$ a \emph{pattern} for
$\sigma$. A pattern is determined by recording, for each point of $Z$,
whether it lies in $U$, in one of the $t$ generators, or in none of them,
so there are at most $(t+2)^{M}$ patterns. Some pattern $(U;X_1,\dots,X_t)$
is therefore a pattern for at least $n!/(t+2)^{M}$ permutations. For each
such $\sigma$ the family $\sigma\bigl(Q(U\cup R;X_1,\dots,X_t)\bigr)$ is
monochromatic, so the shape $u=|U|+r$, $a_i=|X_i|$ has
$\rho_\chi(u;a)\ge(t+2)^{-M}$. The constraints on $u$ and the $a_i$ hold
because $U,X_1,\dots,X_t$ are disjoint subsets of $Z$ and the $X_i$ are
nonempty.
\end{proof}

The lower bound $(t+2)^{-M}$ depends on $t$ alone. The price paid for it
is that the base size $u$ is only known to lie in a window of length $M$
above $r$; the block chains of the next section are designed to absorb
exactly this uncertainty.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}}
\label{sec:lower}

Fix $t\ge1$, put $M=M(t)$, and let $n$ be large enough that
\[
m=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n-1}{M}\Bigr\rfloor\qquad\text{and}\qquad
s=\Bigl\lfloor\frac m2\Bigr\rfloor
\]
satisfy $s\ge t$. Note that $mM\le n-1$. Fix pairwise disjoint sets
$G_1,\dots,G_m\subseteq[n]$ of size $M$, the \emph{slots}, and list each
slot as $G_k=\{g_{k,1},\dots,g_{k,M}\}$; for instance
$g_{k,i}=(k-1)M+i$. For a vector $\beta\in[M]^{m}$ of block sizes let
\[
Y_k^{\beta}=\{g_{k,1},\dots,g_{k,\beta_k}\}\qquad(1\le k\le m),
\]
and for $\sigma\in S_n$ let $Q_{\sigma,\beta}(j,A)$ denote the cube
$Q(j,A)$ of Definition~\ref{def:chain} built from the blocks
$\sigma(Y_1^{\beta}),\dots,\sigma(Y_m^{\beta})$. Thus
$Q_{\sigma,\beta}(j,A)=\sigma\bigl(Q_{\mathrm{id},\beta}(j,A)\bigr)$, and
$Q_{\sigma,\beta}(j,A)$ has shape
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chainshape}
\Bigl(\sum_{k\le j,\ k\notin A}\beta_k;\ (\beta_a)_{a\in A}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Let $\cP$ be the set of pairs $(j,A)$ with $s<j\le m$, $A\subseteq[s]$
and $|A|=t$. Call $\beta$ \emph{good for} $(j,A)$ if the shape
\eqref{eq:chainshape} satisfies $\rho_\chi\ge(t+2)^{-M}$.

\begin{lemma}[Slices]
\label{lem:slices}
Let $1\le j\le m$ and $A\subseteq[j-1]$ with $|A|=t$. At least
$M^{m-t-1}$ vectors $\beta\in[M]^{m}$ are good for $(j,A)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $A=\{a_{(1)}<\dots<a_{(t)}\}$ and let $K=[m]\setminus(A\cup\{j\})$,
a set of $m-t-1$ indices. We show that every choice of $(\beta_k)_{k\in
K}\in[M]^{K}$ extends to a vector $\beta$ that is good for $(j,A)$.
Different choices give different vectors, which proves the lemma.

Fix $(\beta_k)_{k\in K}$, let $R=\sum_{k<j,\,k\notin A}\beta_k$, which
involves only indices in $K$, and put $r=R+1$. Since $R\le(j-1-t)M\le
(m-2)M$, we have $r+M\le(m-1)M+1\le n$. Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation}
gives $u$ and $a_1,\dots,a_t$ with $\rho_\chi(u;a)\ge(t+2)^{-M}$, where
$z=u-r$ satisfies $0\le z\le M-t\le M-1$ and $1\le a_i\le M$. Set
\[
\beta_j=z+1,\qquad \beta_{a_{(i)}}=a_i\quad(1\le i\le t).
\]
These values lie in $[M]$, and the shape \eqref{eq:chainshape} becomes
$(R+z+1;a_1,\dots,a_t)=(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)$. So $\beta$ is good for
$(j,A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Averaging]
\label{lem:average}
There are $\sigma\in S_n$ and $\beta\in[M]^{m}$ such that at least
\[
\frac{|\cP|}{(t+2)^{M}M^{t+1}}
\]
of the cubes $Q_{\sigma,\beta}(j,A)$, $(j,A)\in\cP$, are monochromatic.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Count the triples $(\sigma,\beta,(j,A))\in S_n\times[M]^m\times\cP$ for
which $Q_{\sigma,\beta}(j,A)$ is monochromatic. For fixed $(j,A)\in\cP$
and $\beta$ good for $(j,A)$, the number of suitable $\sigma$ is $n!$
times the value of $\rho_\chi$ at the shape \eqref{eq:chainshape}, which
is at least $n!\,(t+2)^{-M}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:slices} the number of
triples is at least $|\cP|\,M^{m-t-1}\,n!\,(t+2)^{-M}$. There are
$n!\,M^{m}$ pairs $(\sigma,\beta)$, so one of them occurs in at least
$|\cP|\,(t+2)^{-M}M^{-t-1}$ triples.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}]
Let $c_t$ be as in the statement. Since $M\ge t+1$ we have
$c_t\le(4M(t+1))^{-(t+1)}$, so for $n<4M(t+1)$ the bound
$F(n)\ge1>c_tn^{t+1}$ is trivial. Let $n\ge4M(t+1)$.

Take $\sigma,\beta$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:average} and apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:glue} to the blocks
$\sigma(Y_1^\beta),\dots,\sigma(Y_m^\beta)$, which are pairwise disjoint
and nonempty, and to the set $\cS\subseteq\cP$ of pairs whose cube is
monochromatic. Every colouring therefore has a monochromatic union-closed
family with at least
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pbound}
\frac{|\cP|}{2\,(t+2)^{M}M^{t+1}}
\end{equation}
members. It remains to bound $|\cP|=(m-s)\binom st$ from below. We have
$m\ge(n-M)/M$, so $s\ge(m-1)/2\ge n/(2M)-1\ge n/(4M)$ because $n\ge4M$.
In particular $s\ge t+1$, so $\binom st\ge(s/t)^{t}$, and $m-s\ge s$.
Hence
\[
|\cP|\ \ge\ \frac{s^{t+1}}{t^{t}}\ \ge\
\frac{n^{t+1}}{t^{t}(4M)^{t+1}},
\]
and \eqref{eq:Pbound} is at least $c_t\,n^{t+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:growth}
The proof gives an explicit, if very slowly growing, function in
Corollary~\ref{cor:conj}(i). By Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, $F(n)\ge n^{t}$
as soon as $n\ge1/c_t$. For $t=1$ we have $1/c_1=4608<2^{16}$, and for
$t\ge2$ a crude estimate using $t+1\le M(t)$ and $M(t)\ge18$ gives
$\log_2(1/c_t)\le1+2M+4M\log_2(M+1)\le2^{M+2}$ with $M=M(t)$. So
$F(n)\ge n^{t}$ whenever $\log_2\log_2n\ge M(t)+2$. Let $E(x)=2^{x}$, let
$E^{k}$ be its $k$-fold iterate, and let $\log^{*}n$ be the least $k$
with $E^{k}(0)\ge n$. Since $x+2^{2^{x}}\le E^{3}(x)$ for $x\ge0$,
\eqref{eq:Mt} gives $M(t)\le E^{3t}(0)$, and the condition above holds
once $n\ge E^{3t+3}(0)$. Consequently the function $\omega$ constructed
in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:conj}(i) satisfies
$\omega(n)\ge\frac13\log^{*}n-2$. We have made no attempt to improve
this, and it is very far from the limit $(1+o(1))\log_2n$ of
Corollary~\ref{cor:conj}(iii).
\end{remark}

\section{The upper bound}
\label{sec:upper}

A family $A_1,\dots,A_d$ of subsets of $[n]$ is an \emph{independent
system} if each $A_i$ has a \emph{private element}, that is, some
$x_i\in A_i$ lying in no $A_{i'}$ with $i'\ne i$. A set $X\subseteq[n]$ is
\emph{shattered} by a family $\cF$ if $\{A\cap X:\ A\in\cF\}=2^{X}$, and
the \emph{Vapnik--Chervonenkis dimension} $\vc(\cF)$ is the largest size of
a shattered set.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:indep}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)},leftmargin=2em]
\item If $A_1,\dots,A_d$ is an independent system, the $2^{d}-1$ unions
$\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i$, $\emptyset\ne I\subseteq[d]$, are pairwise
distinct.
\item If $\cF$ shatters a set of size $d$, then $\cF$ contains an
independent system of $d$ sets.
\item \textup{(Sauer \cite{Sa72}, Shelah \cite{Sh72})} If $\vc(\cF)\le
d$, then $|\cF|\le\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom nj$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) The private element $x_i$ lies in $\bigcup_{i'\in I}A_{i'}$ exactly
when $i\in I$, so the union determines $I$. (b) If $X=\{x_1,\dots,x_d\}$
is shattered, choose $A_i\in\cF$ with $A_i\cap X=\{x_i\}$; then $x_i$ is a
private element of $A_i$. (c) This is the Sauer--Shelah lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:upper}]
Let $\chi$ be a uniformly random $2$-colouring of $2^{[n]}$, the colours
of the $2^{n}$ sets being independent and uniform. Call an ordered
$d$-tuple $(A_1,\dots,A_d)$ of subsets of $[n]$ \emph{bad} if it is an
independent system and the $2^{d}-1$ sets $\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i$,
$\emptyset\ne I\subseteq[d]$, all have the same colour. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:indep}(a) these sets are distinct, so a fixed independent
tuple is bad with probability $2^{1-(2^d-1)}=2^{2-2^{d}}$. There are at
most $2^{nd}$ tuples, and $2^{nd}\cdot2^{2-2^{d}}<1$ by hypothesis, so
some colouring has no bad tuple. Fix it.

Let $\cF$ be a monochromatic union-closed family. If $\cF$ contained an
independent system of $d$ sets, all unions of its members would lie in
$\cF$ and have the same colour, and the system would be bad. So $\cF$
contains no independent system of $d$ sets, $\vc(\cF)\le d-1$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:indep}(b), and $|\cF|\le\sum_{j<d}\binom nj$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:indep}(c).

For the second assertion let $n\ge32$ and
$d=\lceil\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n\rceil+1$. Then $2^{d}\ge2n\log_2n$, while
$nd+2\le n(\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n+2)+2$. The required inequality
$2n\log_2n>n\log_2n+n\log_2\log_2n+2n+2$ is equivalent to
$\log_2n-\log_2\log_2n>2+2/n$, which holds for $n\ge32$: at $n=32$ the
left side is $5-\log_25>2.67$, and it increases with $n$. Finally
$\binom nj\le n^{j}$, so $\sum_{j<d}\binom nj\le d\,n^{d-1}$, and
$d\le\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n+2$.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{fig:surface} shows the size of the bound of
Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} as a function of $n$ and $d$. Taking for $d$ the
least admissible value $d(n)$ gives a bound valid for every $n\ge1$; for
instance $d(n)=6$ for $6\le n\le10$, and $d(n)=\log_2n+\log_2\log_2n+O(1)$
as $n\to\infty$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-surface.tex}
\caption{The surface $\log_2\sum_{j<d}\binom nj$ over $8\le n\le256$ and
$1\le d\le14$, with the plane $z=n$ of the trivial bound $2^{n}$. The red

along which the bound stays far below the plane.}
\label{fig:surface}
\end{figure}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:conj}]
(i) For $n\ge1$ let $\omega(n)$ be the largest integer $c\in\{0,\dots,n\}$
with $n^{c}\le F(n)$; it exists because $F(n)\ge1$. Given $c\ge1$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} with $t=c$ gives $F(n)\ge c_c\,n^{c+1}\ge n^{c}$
for all $n\ge\max(c,1/c_c)$, and for these $n$ we have $\omega(n)\ge c$.
Hence $\omega(n)\to\infty$.

(ii) Put $\eps(n)=F(n)^{1/n}-1+1/n$. Then
$(1+\eps(n))^{n}=(F(n)^{1/n}+1/n)^{n}>F(n)$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:upper},
$\log F(n)=O((\log n)^{2})$, so $F(n)^{1/n}\to1$ and $\eps(n)\to0$.

(iii) If $n^{\omega(n)}\le F(n)$, Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} gives
$\omega(n)\log_2n\le\log_2F(n)\le(1+o(1))(\log_2n)^{2}$.
\end{proof}

The random colouring does contain large monochromatic union-closed
families, and it is worth seeing how large, since this indicates how much
room is left between the two theorems.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:random}
Let $\chi$ be a uniformly random $2$-colouring of $2^{[n]}$. The largest
monochromatic union-closed family has expected size at least
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom nj\,2^{1-2^{j}}\ \ge\ \frac2n\Bigl(\frac{n}{j_0}\Bigr)^{j_0}
\ge n^{\log_2\log_2n-2-o(1)},\qquad j_0=\lfloor\log_2\log_2n\rfloor ,
\]
the inequality holding for $n\ge4$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $c=\chi([n])$ and let $\cY$ be the set of $Y\subseteq[n]$ such that
$\chi([n]\setminus Y')=c$ for every $Y'\subseteq Y$. Then $\cY$ is closed
under taking subsets and under intersection, so
$\cF=\{[n]\setminus Y:\ Y\in\cY\}$ is monochromatic and union-closed,
because $([n]\setminus Y)\cup([n]\setminus Y')=[n]\setminus(Y\cap Y')$.
A set $Y$ of size $j$ lies in $\cY$ exactly when the $2^{j}-1$ distinct
sets $[n]\setminus Y'$ with $\emptyset\ne Y'\subseteq Y$ all have colour
$c$, which happens with probability $2^{1-2^{j}}$. Summing over $Y$ gives
$\Ex|\cF|$. For $n\ge4$ we have $j_0\ge1$ and $2^{j_0}\le\log_2n$, so the
term $j=j_0$ is at least $(2/n)\binom{n}{j_0}\ge(2/n)(n/j_0)^{j_0}$, and
$j_0^{\,j_0}=n^{o(1)}$.
\end{proof}

So for the random colouring the exponent $\omega$ is at least about
$\log_2\log_2n$, while Theorem~\ref{thm:upper}, which is also proved with
the random colouring, allows it to be as large as $\log_2n$. We do not
know where the truth lies (Question~\ref{q:F}).

\section{Cardinality colourings, Hilbert cubes and arithmetic progressions}
\label{sec:card}

In this section $\chi(S)=g(|S|)$ for a function
$g:\{0,1,\dots,n\}\to\{0,1\}$. A $t$-cube of shape $(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)$
consists of sets whose sizes are the elements of $H(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)$, and
every permutation preserves sizes, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rho01}
\rho_\chi(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)=
\begin{cases}1&\text{if $g$ is constant on }H(u;a_1,\dots,a_t),\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:hilbert} shows a monochromatic Hilbert cube of dimension
two.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-hilbert.tex}
\caption{A colouring $g$ of $\{0,1,\dots,20\}$, red discs and blue
squares, and a red Hilbert cube $H(5;3,7)=\{5,8,12,15\}$. For the
cardinality colouring $\chi(S)=g(|S|)$, every $2$-cube of shape $(5;3,7)$
is red.}
\label{fig:hilbert}
\end{figure}

Applied to the colouring $S\mapsto g(r+|S|)$ of $2^{[M]}$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:hjcube} gives the two-colour case of Hilbert's lemma in the
following windowed form.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:hilbertlemma}
Let $t\ge1$, $M=M(t)$ and $r\ge0$. Every $g:\{r,r+1,\dots,r+M\}\to\{0,1\}$
is constant on some Hilbert cube $H(u;a_1,\dots,a_t)$ with $a_i\ge1$,
$u\ge r$ and $u+a_1+\dots+a_t\le r+M$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hilbert}]
Consider the cardinality colouring $\chi(S)=g(|S|)$ and the construction
of Section~\ref{sec:lower}, whose hypotheses hold since $n\ge4M(t+1)$
gives $s\ge n/(4M)\ge t+1$. By \eqref{eq:rho01}, a vector $\beta$ is good
for $(j,A)$ precisely when $g$ is constant on the Hilbert cube formed by
the sizes of the members of $Q_{\mathrm{id},\beta}(j,A)$, and these sizes
are
\[
|C_j\setminus Y_B|=\beta_1+\dots+\beta_j-\sum_{b\in B}\beta_b
\qquad(B\subseteq A),
\]
that is, the elements of $H_\beta(j,A)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:slices}, for
every $(j,A)\in\cP$ at least $M^{m-t-1}$ vectors $\beta$ make
$H_\beta(j,A)$ monochromatic. Averaging over the $M^{m}$ vectors, as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:average} but without the permutations, some $\beta$ makes
at least $|\cP|/M^{t+1}$ of the cubes $H_\beta(j,A)$ monochromatic, and
at least half of these share a colour $c$. The estimate
$|\cP|\ge n^{t+1}/(t^{t}(4M)^{t+1})$ from the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} completes the proof. All elements of the cubes
lie in $\{0,\dots,n\}$, since $\beta_1+\dots+\beta_m\le mM\le n-1$.
\end{proof}

Given $\beta$ and $c$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:hilbert}, the cubes
$Q_{\mathrm{id},\beta}(j,A)$ attached to the pairs of colour $c$ are
monochromatic under $\chi$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:glue} glues them
into a single union-closed family of colour $c$. For cardinality
colourings the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} is therefore entirely a
statement about sums of a bounded sequence of integers, and the factor
$(t+2)^{-M}$ coming from permutations disappears.

There is also a direct route through arithmetic progressions, which gives
Howorka's theorem with different constants. Let $W(k)$ be the van der
Waerden number, the least $N$ such that every $2$-colouring of
$\{1,\dots,N\}$ contains a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length
$k$; it is finite by van der Waerden's theorem \cite{vdW27}, and Shelah
\cite{Sh88} showed that it is bounded by a primitive recursive function
of $k$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:AP}
Suppose that $g$ is constant on the arithmetic progression
$a,a+e,\dots,a+(k-1)e$, where $e\ge1$, $k\ge1$ and $a+(k-1)e\le n$. Put
$q=\lfloor a/e\rfloor$ and $b=q+k-1$. Then $\chi$ has a monochromatic
union-closed family with $\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom bi$ members and a
monochromatic sublattice with $2^{k-1}$ members.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $a_0=a-qe$, so $0\le a_0<e$, and choose pairwise disjoint sets
$D,X_1,\dots,X_b\subseteq[n]$ with $|D|=a_0$ and $|X_i|=e$; this is
possible because $a_0+be=a+(k-1)e\le n$. The family
\[
\cF=\bigl\{D\cup X_I:\ I\subseteq[b],\ |I|\ge q\bigr\}
\]
is union-closed, since $|I\cup I'|\ge q$ whenever $|I|\ge q$. A member
with index set $I$ has size $a_0+|I|e=a+(|I|-q)e$, where $0\le|I|-q\le
k-1$, so all members have the colour of the progression, and
$|\cF|=\sum_{i=q}^{b}\binom bi=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom bi$. For the
sublattice take the $(k-1)$-cube $Q(D';X_1,\dots,X_{k-1})$ with $|D'|=a$
and $|X_i|=e$, whose member sizes $a+|I|e$ lie in the progression.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Howorka]
\label{thm:howorka}
Let $k\ge2$ and $n\ge2W(k)$. Every cardinality colouring of $2^{[n]}$ has
a monochromatic union-closed family with at least
\[
\binom{\lfloor n/(2W(k))\rfloor+k-1}{k-1}\ \ge\
\Bigl(\frac{n}{2(k-1)W(k)}\Bigr)^{k-1}
\]
members. Consequently there is $\omega(n)\to\infty$ such that every
cardinality colouring of $2^{[n]}$ has a monochromatic union-closed
family with at least $n^{\omega(n)}$ members.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $W=W(k)$ and $m_0=\lceil n/2\rceil$. The integers
$m_0,m_0+1,\dots,m_0+W-1$ lie in $\{0,\dots,n\}$ because $n\ge2W$, so $g$
is constant on a progression $a,a+e,\dots,a+(k-1)e$ among them. Here
$a\ge n/2$ and $(k-1)e\le W-1$, so $e<W$ and $\lfloor a/e\rfloor\ge\lfloor
n/(2W)\rfloor$. Proposition~\ref{prop:AP} gives a monochromatic
union-closed family with at least $\binom b{k-1}$ members, where
$b\ge\lfloor n/(2W)\rfloor+k-1\ge n/(2W)$, and $\binom
b{k-1}\ge(b/(k-1))^{k-1}$. For the last statement let $\omega(n)=k/3$,
where $k$ is the largest integer with $k\ge4$ and $2(k-1)W(k)\le
n^{1/2}$, and let $\omega(n)=0$ if there is no such $k$. The bound is
then at least $n^{(k-1)/2}\ge n^{k/3}$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is attributed to Howorka in \cite{Bl26} without a
reference, and as we have not found Howorka's proof in print we have
included the short argument above. Proposition~\ref{prop:AP} also restricts the
cardinality colourings that are extremal for $f$. If no monochromatic
sublattice has more than $\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil$ members, then $g$ has no
monochromatic progression of length $k$ with
$2^{k-1}>\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil$. This condition was used in the search
for the colouring $g_{10}$ of Section~\ref{sec:small}.

\section{The lattice function}
\label{sec:f}

The lower bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:f} is \eqref{eq:trivial}. The upper
bounds come from a random colouring and a count of sublattices, which in
turn rests on the structure of a finite distributive lattice.

Let $\cL\subseteq2^{[n]}$ be a sublattice with $|\cL|\ge2$, and put
$B_0=\bigcap_{A\in\cL}A$ and $T_0=\bigcup_{A\in\cL}A$, both members of
$\cL$. For $x\in T_0\setminus B_0$ let
$A_x=\bigcap\{A\in\cL:\ x\in A\}$, the least member of $\cL$ containing
$x$, and let $\cJ=\cJ(\cL)=\{A_x:\ x\in T_0\setminus B_0\}$, ordered by
inclusion. A \emph{down-set} of a poset $P$ is a set $D\subseteq P$ with
$y\le x\in D\Rightarrow y\in D$; we write $\Down(P)$ for the set of
down-sets and $\wdt(P)$ for the largest size of an antichain.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:structure}
Let $\cL$ be a sublattice of $2^{[n]}$ with $|\cL|\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)},leftmargin=2em]
\item Every $A\in\cL$ equals $B_0\cup\bigcup\{J\in\cJ:\ J\subseteq A\}$.
\item If $J\in\cJ$ and $J\subseteq A_1\cup\dots\cup A_r$ with
$A_1,\dots,A_r\in\cL$, then $J\subseteq A_i$ for some $i$.
\item The map $A\mapsto\{J\in\cJ:\ J\subseteq A\}$ is a bijection from
$\cL$ onto $\Down(\cJ)$.
\item $1\le|\cJ|\le n$ and $\wdt(\cJ)\le\log_2|\cL|$.
\item Let $\psi:[n]\to\cJ\cup\{\bot,\top\}$ be given by $\psi(x)=\bot$ for
$x\in B_0$, $\psi(x)=\top$ for $x\notin T_0$, and $\psi(x)=A_x$
otherwise. Then $\cL$ consists of the sets
$\{x:\ \psi(x)=\bot\text{ or }\psi(x)\in D\}$, $D\in\Down(\cJ)$. So $\cL$
is determined by the poset $\cJ$, given abstractly on a set of labels,
together with $\psi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) If $x\in A\setminus B_0$ then $A_x\subseteq A$, so $A$ is contained
in the right-hand side, and the reverse inclusion is clear.

(b) Let $J=A_x$. Then $x\in A_i$ for some $i$, and $A_x\subseteq A_i$ by
minimality.

(c) The map is injective by (a), and its values are down-sets. Given a
down-set $D$, the set $A_D=B_0\cup\bigcup_{J\in D}J$ lies in $\cL$. If
$J\in\cJ$ and $J\subseteq A_D$, then by (b) either $J\subseteq B_0$, which
is impossible because $J=A_x$ with $x\notin B_0$, or $J\subseteq J'$ for
some $J'\in D$, and then $J\in D$. So $A_D$ is mapped to $D$.

(d) As $|\cL|\ge2$ we have $T_0\ne B_0$, so $\cJ\ne\emptyset$. List $\cJ$
as $J_1,\dots,J_k$ in an order compatible with inclusion. The sets
$\{J_1,\dots,J_i\}$, $0\le i\le k$, are down-sets, so by (c) they
correspond to a strictly increasing chain of $k+1$ subsets of $[n]$, and
$k\le n$. If $S\subseteq\cJ$ is an antichain, the down-sets generated by
the $2^{|S|}$ subsets of $S$ are distinct, so $2^{|S|}\le|\cL|$.

(e) For $x\in T_0\setminus B_0$ and $J\in\cJ$ we have $x\in J$ if and
only if $A_x\subseteq J$. Hence $x\in A_D$ if and only if $A_x\subseteq J$
for some $J\in D$, which for a down-set $D$ means $A_x\in D$.
\end{proof}

Part (c) is Birkhoff's representation of finite distributive lattices
\cite{Bi37}, specialised to sublattices of $2^{[n]}$.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:posets}
Let $k\ge1$ and $w\ge1$. The number of partial orders of width at most $w$ on the labelled set
$[k]$ is at most $(k+1)^{k(w+2)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $v=\min(\lfloor w\rfloor,k)$. The width of the poset is at most $v$,
so by Dilworth's theorem \cite{Di50} it is the union of $v$ disjoint
chains $\Gamma_1,\dots,\Gamma_v$, some possibly empty. Record for each
$x\in[k]$ the index $c(x)$ of its chain, its position $p(x)$ in that
chain counted from the bottom, and for each $i\le v$ the number $r_i(x)$
of elements of $\Gamma_i$ below or equal to $x$. The elements of
$\Gamma_i$ below or equal to $x$ form an initial segment of $\Gamma_i$,
so $y\le x$ holds exactly when $p(y)\le r_{c(y)}(x)$. The data determine
the order, and there are at most $v^{k}k^{k}(k+1)^{kv}\le(k+1)^{k(v+2)}
\le(k+1)^{k(w+2)}$ possibilities.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:count}
For $m\ge2$ the number $N(m)$ of sublattices of $2^{[n]}$ with exactly $m$
members satisfies $N(m)\le(n+2)^{n(\log_2m+4)}$. The number of
sublattices with at least two members is at most $2^{n(n+1)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:structure}(d),(e), a sublattice with $m$ members is
determined by a partial order of width at most $\log_2m$ on a label set
$[k]$ with $k\le n$, together with a map $[n]\to[k]\cup\{\bot,\top\}$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:posets} gives
$N(m)\le\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k+1)^{k(\log_2m+2)}(k+2)^{n}\le
n\,(n+2)^{n(\log_2m+3)}\le(n+2)^{n(\log_2m+4)}$. For the second
statement, Lemma~\ref{lem:structure}(a) shows that $\cL$ consists of the
unions of $B_0$ with subfamilies of $\cJ$, so $\cL$ is determined by the
family $\{B_0\}\cup\cJ$ of at most $n+1$ sets, in which $B_0$ is the
smallest member. Such a family can be
written as a sequence of exactly $n+1$ subsets of $[n]$, repeating a
member if necessary, and there are $2^{n(n+1)}$ such sequences.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:f}]
Let $\chi$ be a uniformly random colouring. A fixed family of $m$ sets is
monochromatic with probability $2^{1-m}$.

For the first bound put $m_1=n^{2}+n+2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:count} the
expected number of monochromatic sublattices with at least $m_1$ members
is at most $2^{n(n+1)}2^{1-m_1}=1/2$, so some colouring has none, and
$f(n)\le m_1-1$.

For the second bound we may assume $n\ge3$, since for $n\le2$ it is
weaker than $f(n)\le2^{n}$. Put $\ell=\log_2(n+2)$, so $\ell>2.32$, and
$L=n\ell$ and $m_2=\lceil12n\ell^{2}\rceil$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:count},
$N(m)2^{1-m}\le2^{L(\log_2m+4)+1-m}$ for $m\ge2$. The function
$\varphi(m)=m/2-L(\log_2m+4)-1$ is increasing for $m>2L/\ln2$, and
$m_2>2.9L>2L/\ln2$, so $\varphi(m)\ge\varphi(m_2)$ for $m\ge m_2$. Using
$m_2\le13n\ell^{2}$, $\log_2n\le\ell$ and $\log_2\ell\le\ell-1$, which
holds for $\ell\ge2$, we get $\log_2m_2+4\le\log_213+3\ell+2<3\ell+5.71$,
hence
\[
L(\log_2m_2+4)+1\ \le\ 3n\ell^{2}+5.71\,n\ell+1\ \le\ 6n\ell^{2}\ \le\
m_2/2,
\]
the middle inequality being $3\ell\ge5.71+1/(n\ell)$, true for
$\ell>2.32$. So $\varphi(m_2)\ge0$, and $N(m)2^{1-m}\le2^{-m/2}$ for
all $m\ge m_2$. The expected number of monochromatic sublattices with at
least $m_2$ members is then at most
$\sum_{m\ge m_2}2^{-m/2}=2^{-m_2/2}/(1-2^{-1/2})<1$, and $f(n)\le
m_2-1<12n\ell^{2}$.

The last assertion of the theorem follows from these bounds and
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}.
\end{proof}

The first bound is the better one for $n$ up to about $1300$.
Neither can be pushed below $n$ by studying the random colouring alone.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:chain}
With probability greater than $1/2$, a uniformly random $2$-colouring of
$2^{[n]}$ has a monochromatic chain of $n$ sets, and hence a monochromatic
sublattice of $n$ members.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Build the chain greedily. Put $S_0=\emptyset$ and $c=\chi(\emptyset)$.
Given $S_i$ with $|S_i|=i\le n-2$ and colour $c$, the $n-i$ sets
$S_i\cup\{x\}$, $x\notin S_i$, have not been looked at before, and their
colours are independent and uniform. If one of them has colour $c$, call
it $S_{i+1}$; the step fails with probability $2^{-(n-i)}$. The
probability that some step fails is at most
$\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}2^{-(n-i)}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}2^{-j}<1/2$, and otherwise
$S_0\subset S_1\subset\dots\subset S_{n-1}$ is a monochromatic chain.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact values for small \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}}
\label{sec:small}

For small $n$ both functions can be computed. Fix $n$ and a target $m$.
A colouring with no monochromatic closed family of at least $m$ members
(closed meaning closed under union for $F$, and under union and
intersection for $f$) is a satisfying assignment of the Boolean formula
in the variables $x_S$, $S\subseteq[n]$, that contains, for every closed
family $\cF$ with at least $m$ members, the two clauses
$\bigvee_{S\in\cF}x_S$ and $\bigvee_{S\in\cF}\neg x_S$. These families are far too numerous to list,
so we add them only when needed. A SAT solver (CaDiCaL, called through
PySAT \cite{IMM18}) proposes a colouring that satisfies the clauses
collected so far, and a branch-and-bound search looks in each colour class
for a closed family of at least $m$ members. If it finds one, the two
clauses for that family are added and the solver is called again. If the
search finds nothing, the colouring shows that the value is less than
$m$; if the solver reports that the formula is unsatisfiable, every
colouring has a monochromatic closed family of at least $m$ members. The
unit clause $x_\emptyset$ removes the symmetry $\chi\mapsto1-\chi$, and
letting $m$ increase gives the exact value.

\section{Formal verification}
\label{sec:lean}

The following statements have been formalised and checked in Lean~4
\cite{MU21} with the Mathlib library \cite{Mathlib20}, version~4.34.1 of
both. The development contains no unproved steps, and for each statement
below Lean reports that the proof uses only the three standard axioms
(propositional extensionality, the axiom of choice and the soundness of
quotient types). In the formalisation the ground set is the type of $n$
elements, a colouring is a Boolean function on its subsets, and $F(n)$
and $f(n)$ are defined literally as minima over all colourings of the
size of the largest monochromatic family closed under union, respectively
under union and intersection. It is also checked that $F(n)$ is the
largest $m$ such that every colouring has a monochromatic union-closed
family of at least $m$ sets.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item $\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil\le f(n)\le F(n)$ for every $n$.
\item $F(n)\le\sum_{j<d}\binom nj$ whenever $nd+2<2^{d}$; and $F(n)\le
d\,n^{d}$ for $n\ge2$ with $d=2(\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor+1)$.
\item For every $\eps>0$, $F(n)<(1+\eps)^{n}$ for all large $n$; and
$F(n)<2^{n}$ for $n\ge1$.
\item For every $t\ge1$ there are positive integers $M$ and $K$ such that
\[
\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\lfloor(n-1)/M\rfloor}{2}\Bigr\rfloor+1-t\Bigr)^{t+1}
\le K\,F(n)\qquad(n\ge1),
\]
with subtraction truncated at zero. This is Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} with
a constant of the same kind.
\item For every $c\in\mathbb{N}$, $n^{c}<F(n)$ for all large $n$.
\item The first conjecture: there is $\omega:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$ with
$\omega(n)\to\infty$ and $n^{\omega(n)}\le F(n)$ for every $n$. The second
conjecture: there is $\eps:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $\eps(n)\to0$
and $F(n)<(1+\eps(n))^{n}$ for every $n\ge1$. Their conjunction is proved
as a single theorem.
\end{enumerate}

The formal proof of (4) follows Sections~\ref{sec:cubes}--\ref{sec:lower}
with two changes. The cube lemma is replaced by Mathlib's Hales--Jewett
theorem, as explained in Remark~\ref{rem:hj}, and the probabilistic
language is replaced by counting: permutations and size vectors are
counted rather than sampled, and Lemma~\ref{lem:average} becomes a double
counting over the product of the two sets. The random colouring in (2) is
replaced in the same way by a count of colourings. Theorem~\ref{thm:f}
apart from the trivial lower bound, Propositions~\ref{prop:random}
and~\ref{prop:chain}, the results of Section~\ref{sec:card},
Remark~\ref{rem:growth} and the computations of Section~\ref{sec:small}
are not formalised.

\section{Questions}
\label{sec:questions}

\begin{question}
\label{q:F}
What is the order of magnitude of $\log F(n)/\log n$? By
Corollary~\ref{cor:conj} and Remark~\ref{rem:growth} it tends to infinity
and is at most $(1+o(1))\log_2n$. Is $F(n)=n^{\Theta(\log n)}$? For the
random colouring the answer lies between $\log_2\log_2n$ and $\log_2n$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:random} and the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:upper},
and even there we do not know which end is closer to the truth.
\end{question}

\begin{question}
Is $f(n)=O(n)$? Is $f(n)=\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil$ for every $n$?
Proposition~\ref{prop:chain} shows that the random colouring cannot
settle the first question.
\end{question}

\begin{question}
Can the factor $(t+2)^{-M(t)}$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation} be
improved substantially? A density version of Lemma~\ref{lem:hjcube} with
a good bound would improve the growth of $\omega$ in
Remark~\ref{rem:growth}.
\end{question}

The proofs of Theorems~\ref{thm:lower} and~\ref{thm:upper} extend to any
fixed number $k$ of colours with only notational changes: in
\eqref{eq:Mt} the term $2^{2^{M(t-1)}}$ becomes $k^{2^{M(t-1)}}$, the
factor $1/2$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:glue} becomes $1/k$, and in the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} a fixed independent tuple is
monochromatic with probability $k^{2-2^{d}}$. So both conjectures hold for
every fixed number of colours. This extension is not formalised.
